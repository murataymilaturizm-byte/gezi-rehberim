# How Does an Incoming Agency Work? A Guide to Entering the Foreign-Tourist Market (2026)

Sooner or later, every agency selling domestic tours asks the same question: "What if I sold to foreign visitors too?" Same coach, same guide, same route — but a completely different customer and a completely different way of working. Incoming agency work, meaning selling services to visitors who come to Türkiye, is both more accessible and more different than most agencies assume.

This guide is written for agencies considering the move from domestic work to incoming: how the business actually runs, which channels bring customers, what time-zone and language operations really mean, and where pricing and collection diverge from domestic work. A 12-question FAQ closes the guide.

> **In short:** Incoming work arrives through two channels — from a partner agency abroad (B2B) and directly from the traveller (B2C). Their economics and workload differ: B2B brings larger volume at thinner margins and longer payment terms; B2C offers higher margins but places the entire communication load on you. The real difficulty of the transition is not the tour product but the operation: answering questions that arrive from different time zones, in different languages, quickly and in writing.

## Where Does Incoming Work Come From? Two Channels, Two Economics

**Partner agency (B2B).** A tour operator in Germany, Russia, the Gulf or the Balkans sends its customers to Türkiye and hands the local operation to you: meet-and-greet, transfer, tour, guide. In this channel the burden of finding customers isn't yours — but neither is the bargaining power. Price pressure is high and payment is usually post-tour and on terms; in exchange, a single partner can deliver steady volume across a season.

**Direct traveller (B2C).** The visitor is in Türkiye or planning to come; they find you through the map, reviews, social media or a hotel recommendation and write to you directly. Margins are high and there is no intermediary — but the entire communication load sits with you: answering questions, quoting, booking, reminding.

Most healthy incoming agencies run both: B2B fills the base, B2C lifts the margin. If you are starting out, the realistic order is usually **B2C first** — because partner relationships require references, and references come from work already done.

## Market Selection: Not Everyone — Someone

The most common mistake in incoming is trying to serve "all foreigners". Every market has different expectations: season, booking habits, group size, payment method, even the hour of day they message.

Choosing a single market to start with has three concrete benefits: your language investment goes to one place, you shape the tour product around that market's expectations, and you accumulate real reviews in that market. Make the choice with data, not feelings — look at how many messages arrived in which language last season. If demand is already knocking, start your answer there.

## The Real Difficulty Is Operations, Not the Tour

In domestic work the customer wakes at the same hour, speaks the same language and plans around the same holiday calendar. In incoming, all three change:

| Difference | Domestic | Incoming |
|---|---|---|
| Message hours | office + evening | **the partner's/traveller's time zone** — 2 a.m. is ordinary |
| Language | one | 2-5, usually with English as common ground |
| Decision speed | days | **hours** — a visitor in-country asks about tomorrow |
| Payment | transfer/cash | international card, transfer, partner terms |
| Written confirmation | nice to have | **mandatory** — a misunderstanding becomes a field accident |

The reading of this table: moving to incoming is not a product decision but a **response-capacity** decision. An agency that replies in the morning to a night message loses far more in incoming than domestically — the visitor's days in the country are limited and the alternative is one tap away. ## Pricing and Collection: Where Do They Diverge?

**Pricing.** In B2B you quote a net rate (you don't see the margin the partner adds); in B2C you quote a list price. Keeping both in one list is the most common source of confusion — the partner sees your B2C price and asks "why am I quoted this?". Keep two separate price lists and record which one went to whom.

**Currency.** Letting a foreign customer grasp the price in their own currency directly affects conversion. Manage the exchange risk too: state which rate the quote uses and how long it is valid.

**Collection.** In B2C, being able to accept international cards is a serious advantage; if you can't, clarify your method in the first messages — payment uncertainty is where incoming bookings most often stall. In B2B, terms and invoicing must be written from the start.

## The First 90 Days: A Realistic Plan

**Month one — foundation.** Prepare one clean English page per tour (programme, included-excluded, meeting point, cancellation terms). Add an English description to your Google business profile. Write ready answers to the 8-10 most frequent questions.

**Month two — visibility and first jobs.** Target reviews: ask your first foreign guests for written reviews — in incoming, reviews come before advertising. Meet hotel receptions and local businesses; a large share of early B2C volume comes from these referrals.

**Month three — routine and partners.** Record how many enquiries came from which market; this data both validates your market choice and strengthens your hand in partner talks. Now you have work to reference — go to partner agencies with that file.

Know the threshold ahead of time: messages arriving in different languages from different time zones eventually exceed what human hours can cover. ## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**1. Do I need a separate licence to work as an incoming agency?**
Travel agency activity is licensed and the scope of authority can vary by group type. Confirm with the relevant authority and your accountant whether your current licence covers incoming activity; this guide is informational.

**2. Can I do incoming work without speaking a foreign language?**
To a limited extent, yes. A once-prepared English tour document, ready replies and a written confirmation template let even a non-fluent agency work steadily. What matters is not live conversation but a verified written setup.

**3. Which is more profitable, B2B or B2C?**
Per unit, B2C; in total, often B2B. B2C has no intermediary and higher margins, but every sale costs communication effort. B2B has thinner margins, yet a single agreement can carry volume all season. The healthy structure is a mix.

**4. How do I find a partner agency?**
Trade fairs, sector associations and your existing supplier network are the first doors. But partners look at one thing: reliable local operations. Without work to reference, the conversation stays weak — which is why producing B2C work first is the faster route.

**5. Which language should I start with?**
The language of your own demand. Look at foreign messages from the last three months and invest where the volume is. General rule: English is the common ground; the second language depends on your region.

**6. How do I keep up with night messages?**
At low volume, an English version of your after-hours greeting is the first step. But a greeting doesn't take bookings; as volume grows you need a layer that answers regardless of the hour and completes the booking. A message waiting overnight is the most expensive waiting message in incoming.

**7. Which currency should I quote in?**
That's a business decision; the communication rule doesn't change: the customer must grasp the price in their own currency. State the rate's validity period and the currency you actually collect in.

**8. Is it hard to take a deposit from a foreign guest?**
The difficulty comes from uncertainty more than method. Write upfront which channels you can accept payment through and repeat it in the confirmation. The fewer questions the payment step raises, the fewer bookings stall.

**9. Are reviews really that important?**
In incoming, yes. For a visitor who doesn't know you and may be seeing the country for the first time, reviews are the strongest available signal. A few positive foreign-language reviews bring more work than an equivalent ad budget.

**10. Should I also handle transfers and meet-and-greet?**
In partner work it is usually part of the package. In B2C it is both a separate revenue line and a powerful first touchpoint — a guest met at the airport tends to buy the tour from you too.

**11. Is there off-season incoming work?**
Depending on the market, yes: cultural, thermal, congress and faith tourism extend beyond summer. We covered off-season planning separately in [our off-season revenue guide](/en/blog/sezon-disi-gelir-stratejileri).

**12. When should I automate?**
The measure is clear: if your record and response routine still works in your busiest week, your current method is enough. If it collapses in every busy period — messages piling up, night enquiries missed, confusion over what you told which partner — the problem is your method, not your effort.
