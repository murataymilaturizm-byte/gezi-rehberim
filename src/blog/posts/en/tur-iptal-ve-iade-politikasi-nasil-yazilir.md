# How to Write a Tour Cancellation & Refund Policy (Template + 2026 Regulatory Notes)

"I want my deposit back." The first time an agency owner meets this sentence, they hold one of two things: a written cancellation-refund policy shared at the moment of sale — or an argument. With a policy, the conversation is short: the terms apply as written, exceptions are granted in writing if needed, and both sides knew the rules from the start. Without one, the conversation stretches, hardens, and however it ends, the loser is usually the agency: money, the customer, or both.

This guide walks through how a tour-selling agency should build its cancellation and refund policy end to end: the general frame the regulations draw, a reasonable deposit-refund ladder example, the principles that apply when the agency itself cancels, where and how the policy must be shown to the customer — and the article's real bookmark value: **a fillable policy skeleton, heading by heading.** A 12-question FAQ closes the guide.

> **In short:** A cancellation-refund policy is not paperwork; it is part of the sale. An unwritten term works against the agency in a dispute; a written term shared upfront prevents the dispute itself. A solid policy stands on four legs: alignment with the general principles of the regulations, a clear refund ladder staged by date, a full-refund-or-alternative principle for agency-caused cancellations, and visibility in every sales channel — on the website, in the catalogue, on WhatsApp together with the price. Always have your own lawyer do the final read.

## Why a Written Policy Is Non-Negotiable: Unwritten Terms Cut Against You

By its nature, a cancellation dispute leaves both sides feeling right: the customer says "the tour is a month away and you're holding my money"; the agency says "I held that seat for you and turned other customers away." Both are true from where they stand — which is exactly why the argument has no fair resolution on verbal ground. A written term moves the dispute from the emotional plane of "who is right" to the factual plane of "what did we agree".

The cost of writing nothing comes in three layers. The first is legal: when a dispute reaches an official channel, the party that failed its written-information duty — the agency — starts at a disadvantage; "I told them verbally" has little evidentiary weight. The second is financial: the policy-less agency usually drifts toward "fine, I'll refund it" just to end the argument — with the seat already burned. The third and most lasting is reputation: the line from [our online sales guide](/en/blog/internetten-tur-satisi-nasil-yapilir) applies here too — uncertainty is the mother of the internet's most expensive reviews. One one-star "they kept my deposit" review costs more than dozens of deposits combined.

This is one of the traps new agencies fall into most often; mistake number six in [10 Mistakes Made When Opening an Agency](/en/blog/acente-acarken-yapilan-10-hata) — price and contract indiscipline — describes exactly this gap. This article is that mistake's "how to fix it" side.

## The Frame the Regulations Draw: General Principles

An honest warning first: this section is for information, not legal advice. Regulations get updated; rates and deadlines change. When writing your policy, **rely on the current text of the regulations** and have a lawyer read the final version. What follows is the unchanging logic of the frame.

In Türkiye, tour sales sit inside consumer-protection law and the rules on package-tour contracts. The general principles that concern an agency directly:

- **Pre-sale information duty:** Before the sale, the tour's scope, what the price includes, the payment plan and the cancellation-refund terms must be presented clearly and provably (in writing or on a durable digital medium). "They could have asked" does not count as informing.
- **Contract requirement:** Package-tour sales rest on a written contract (or its digital equivalent in distance sales); the cancellation-refund terms are part of it.
- **Interpretation in the consumer's favour:** Vague, contradictory or later-added terms are read in the consumer's favour. Your policy is only as strong as its clarity and its upfront sharing.
- **No disproportionate penalties:** Terms that put an excessive burden on the consumer (for example, the full price forfeited in every case) can be deemed unfair and void. Writing it into the contract does not make every clause enforceable.
- **Protection when the agency cancels:** If the agency cancels the tour, the customer's right to get their money back or accept an equivalent alternative is a core principle of the regulations.

The practical meaning: a good policy is not a weapon against the customer but a text where **both sides know what they signed.** A policy harsher than the regulations allow leaves you holding a void piece of paper on the very day you need it most — dispute day.

## The Deposit-Refund Ladder: An Example Structure

The heart of the policy answers one question: "if cancelled X days before departure, how much is refunded?" There is no single correct set of numbers; a reasonable ladder is built from your tour type, supplier terms and season. The table below is **not a tariff the regulations mandate — it is an example structure showing the common logic in the sector.** Set the numbers from your own cost reality:

| Time before departure | Example refund approach | The logic |
|---|---|---|
| Early window (e.g. 30+ days) | Full or near-full refund | The seat resells easily; don't punish early deciders |
| Middle window (e.g. 15-30 days) | Partial deduction (the share paid to suppliers) | Hotel/transport advances are now committed; base the deduction on real cost |
| Near window (e.g. 7-15 days) | Higher deduction | Resale odds drop; the deduction reflects it |
| Final days (e.g. 0-7 days) | No or token refund; **date-change/name-transfer alternative** | The seat is effectively burned; offering alternatives is both fair and reputation-building |

Three rules when writing the ladder: **(1) Make every deduction justifiable** — "the hotel advance is non-refundable" is both fairer and more defensible than "we deduct on principle". **(2) Write the alternatives into the policy** — a date change or transfer to another participant turns most cancellation requests into something other than refund requests. **(3) State the refund route and timeline** — which channel, within what reasonable period; "will be refunded" with no deadline is a fresh door to dispute.

## When the Agency Cancels: Force Majeure and Operational Cancellations

The ladder governs the customer's change of heart. The other side of the coin — cancellations you make — is the section most agencies never write, yet it earns the most trust.

**Weather and safety:** The balloon doesn't fly, the boat trip meets a storm, the road closes. The principle is clear: even when the service fails through no fault of yours, the customer paid for a service they did not receive — a full refund or an alternative the customer accepts (next morning, another date, an equivalent tour) is offered. "The weather isn't our fault, no refund" clashes with the regulatory principles and is the source of the most destructive reviews.

**Insufficient participation:** Failing to reach a minimum group size can be a legitimate cancellation ground — but only if **written from the start**: state the minimum in the policy, state the latest point at which customers will be notified, and in that case offer a full refund plus, where possible, an alternative tour. A "the group didn't fill" message one day before departure wounds your reputation even if it is written; set the notice threshold generously against yourself.

**Programme changes:** For changes that stop short of cancellation but count as material (accommodation class, route, duration), giving the customer a choice of acceptance or penalty-free withdrawal is a standard aligned with the regulatory principles — and a dispute-preventer.

The summary of this section is one sentence: **when the customer withdraws, the ladder; when you cancel, full-refund-or-alternative.** A policy built on that symmetry reads as a fair contract, not a rigged text — and customers feel it.

## Where to Show the Policy: Writing It Is Not Enough

A policy in a drawer is a policy that doesn't exist. The regulations' "provable information" principle demands as much: it is not enough to have written the terms; you must be able to show they were shared before the sale. Four points are standard:

1. **Website:** A dedicated "Cancellation & Refund Terms" page + a link to it from every tour page. If there is a booking form, add a checkbox confirmation.
2. **Catalogues and price lists:** A short summary at the end of every printed or PDF material + a pointer to the full text.
3. **On WhatsApp, together with the price:** The terms should be a natural part of the price message — quoting the price and mentioning the terms only when asked is the most common mistake. Add a cancellation-refund summary to the quick-reply templates we describe in [our WhatsApp Business setup guide](/en/blog/acenteler-icin-whatsapp-business-kurulumu); instead of wondering "should I paste the terms", send them with one tap.
4. **In the booking confirmation:** The written confirmation sent when the deposit arrives should carry the full terms or their link once more. That is the record that closes the "nobody told me" objection for good.

As volume grows, keeping this discipline by hand gets hard: on a busy day the price goes out fast and the terms get forgotten — and the one forgotten chat resurfaces months later as a dispute. The quiet benefit of systems that run the booking conversation end to end is exactly here: the cancellation-refund terms go out with every price, in every chat, at every hour, **automatically and identically.** The best way to win the argument is for the argument never to be born.

## The Ready Skeleton: Fill It In Heading by Heading

The skeleton below is the structure to follow when writing your own policy. Replace the brackets with your details; before publishing, **have your own lawyer/accountant do the final read** — this skeleton is a starting point, not legal advice.

**[Agency Name] Tour Cancellation & Refund Policy** *(Last updated: [date])*

1. **Scope:** This policy applies to [day tours / overnight tours / all tours] sold by [agency name]. [Exceptions if any: e.g. private groups, international tours.]
2. **Booking and deposit:** A booking is confirmed with a deposit of [amount/rate] per person, payable via [channels]; a written confirmation follows the receipt.
3. **Refund ladder for guest-initiated cancellation:** More than [X] days before departure: [terms]. Between [X]-[Y] days: [terms]. Less than [Y] days: [terms]. *(Adapt the example table above with your own numbers.)*
4. **Date changes and transfers:** One date change is possible [free/conditionally] until [X] days before departure. A booking may be transferred to another participant [under conditions].
5. **Agency-initiated cancellation:** If the tour is cancelled by the agency due to [weather/safety, insufficient participation, etc.], the guest chooses between a full refund and [an alternative date/equivalent tour]. Cancellation for insufficient participation is notified no later than [X days/hours] before departure.
6. **Programme changes:** For material changes the guest is offered acceptance or penalty-free withdrawal. [What counts as material.]
7. **Refund method and timeline:** Refunds are made via the original payment method within [reasonable period] of the cancellation being finalised.
8. **Special cases:** [Visa refusal, medical documentation, name-spelling corrections etc. — how each is handled.]
9. **Contact:** Cancellation-refund requests are submitted in writing via [channel]; verbal requests become effective with written confirmation.

All nine headings fit on a single A4 page — and that one page ends at least one major dispute per season before it begins.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**1. Can I write "the deposit is non-refundable under any circumstances"?**
You can write it, but it is not guaranteed to hold: terms placing a disproportionate burden on the consumer can be deemed unfair and void. A staged ladder with deductions based on real cost is both fairer and far more defensible in a dispute.

**2. We agreed verbally and now the customer wants the deposit back; what can I do?**
The lesson of this round is the rule of the next sale: without written terms the burden of proof is yours, and the practical outcome is usually a refund. For the current case offer a reasonable middle path (date change, partial refund); the real fix is that no sale ever closes without written terms again.

**3. The balloon didn't fly and the customer wants a refund; can I say "the weather isn't our fault"?**
The absence of fault doesn't change the fact that the service wasn't delivered. The standard practice is a full refund or an alternative the customer accepts (next morning, another date). Write it into your policy upfront and a cancelled flight becomes routine, not a crisis.

**4. Is cancelling a tour for insufficient participation my right?**
It can be a legitimate ground if written from the start: state the minimum number and the latest notification date in your policy, and offer a full refund plus an alternative where possible. If it isn't written, the cancellation is entirely your risk.

**5. Within what period should I pay a refund?**
Write a clear, reasonable period into your policy and honour it; for current statutory limits, rely on the text of the regulations. A late refund turns even a case where you were right into a complaint — once a refund is decided, paying fast is the cheapest reputation investment there is.

**6. Won't adding terms to every price message scare customers away?**
The opposite: a seller who states terms upfront signals trust. A two-line summary plus a link to the full text is enough. Customers don't run from terms; they run from terms that appear later.

**7. Does a date change count as a cancellation?**
That depends on your definition — which is why it deserves its own heading in the policy. Making changes easy until a set date turns a cancellation request into a booking-preserving request; that is often the only scenario where both sides win.

**8. Is a refund mandatory for visa refusal or medical grounds?**
Regulate these special cases separately in your policy and confirm the current legal principles with your lawyer. A common reasonable approach is to deduct nothing beyond what was actually paid to suppliers when the documented necessity is genuine.

**9. The policy is on my website; must I also share it on WhatsApp?**
Yes — provable information is established by sharing in the sales channel. Sitting on the website does not by itself show it was presented to that customer before the sale; a summary plus link sent with the price message closes that gap.

**10. Can I change my policy mid-season?**
Going forward, yes; existing bookings keep the terms shared at the time of sale. Trying to apply new terms to an old booking is an invitation to a lost argument — which is why a version/update date on the policy text matters.

**11. Do I need to issue documentation for the amount I deduct?**
The paperwork around collections and refunds depends on your tax regime and business structure — a general rule here would mislead; ask your accountant. The unchanging principle: every collection, every deduction and every refund must match a written record.

**12. Can I fill in this template and use it right away?**
As a skeleton, yes; as a final text, no: your tour types, supplier contracts and the current regulations must shape your version. Fill it in, adapt it to your reality, and have a lawyer read it before publishing — that one-time check secures a text you will use for seasons.
